🧩 Missing values

Missingness is not a single defect with a single remedy. A sound strategy must identify what is truly missing, describe where missingness occurs, investigate what predicts it, and decide how present and future incomplete observations will be handled.

Scope

This guide focuses mainly on predictor missingness. A missing target is different: training observations without the outcome usually cannot contribute to ordinary supervised fitting, while the target is naturally unknown when making a future prediction. A target should not be imputed as though it were an ordinary predictor without a specialized, defensible design.

The aim is not necessarily to produce a table containing no missing markers. The aim is to establish a documented strategy that avoids unjustified information loss, behaves appropriately on future data, and prevents information from validation or test observations leaking into learned preprocessing.

🧾 Begin with the meaning of “missing”

Several superficially similar states require different treatment.

State Meaning Typical response
Missing value A value is unavailable even though the variable is relevant to the observation. Investigate, remove, impute, add an indicator, or use supported native handling.
Placeholder An ordinary-looking value such as -99, N/A, or 1900-01-01 was used to represent absence. Confirm its meaning and convert it to an explicit missing marker.
Structurally absent The variable does not apply, such as a component measurement for an object without that component. Preserve the distinction or apply a documented domain rule; do not automatically treat it as unknown.
Not yet observed The value may arrive later because of workflow or reporting delay. Model the delay, wait, or define a prediction-time cutoff consistently.

The data dictionary and collection process are often more informative than a statistical test. A suspicious code is only a candidate placeholder until its meaning is established. Conversely, converting a genuinely meaningful value to missing can be as damaging as leaving a placeholder untouched.

📋 A practical assessment framework

Assessment should be performed before and after consequential cleaning decisions. Removing incomplete rows first can make the remaining variables look artificially complete and can hide the population that was lost.

1. Identify true missingness

Search for explicit missing markers and plausible placeholders. Compare candidates with variable type, observed range, frequency, source documentation, and neighboring records. Distinguish “not available” from “not applicable.”

When a missing value has a deterministic domain interpretation, a manual rule may be appropriate. For example, if a data specification states that a component count is blank exactly when the component is absent, converting that structural blank to zero may be defensible. The rule should be reproducible and should also be applicable to future observations.

2. Measure prevalence

Summarize missingness by both variable and observation. There is no universal percentage at which a variable or row becomes unusable. The cost depends on sample size, variable importance, the missingness process, available alternatives, and how the final system will respond to incomplete future cases.

Inspect prevalence across meaningful groups as well as overall. A modest global rate can conceal severe missingness in one site, period, class, or population. Sequence plots are particularly valuable when collection practices have changed over time or recent records are still incomplete.

3. Examine patterns and associations

Variables frequently go missing together because they share a form, source, eligibility rule, or operational failure. Missingness sets show exact combinations. Association rules show directional conditional patterns. Observation-level missing counts reveal whether incompleteness is diffuse or concentrated in a subgroup of rows.

Co-occurrence suggests where to investigate; it does not establish that one missing variable caused another. A shared upstream event can generate the entire pattern.

4. Test predictability without over-naming the mechanism

A missingness indicator can be modeled using observed predictors. Good held-out prediction is evidence that the missingness is patterned with respect to the supplied information. Failure to predict may mean the process is close to random, but it can also mean that the relevant cause was not recorded, the model was unsuitable, or the sample was too small.

Formal missing-data mechanisms

The formal MCAR, MAR, and MNAR terms describe assumptions about the missingness process rather than labels that an exploratory chart can usually determine.

Mechanism Informal meaning
Missing completely at random (MCAR) Missingness does not depend on observed values or on the values that are missing.
Missing at random (MAR) After conditioning on the observed information used in the analysis, missingness does not additionally depend on the unseen value itself.
Missing not at random (MNAR) Dependence on unseen values remains after conditioning on observed information.

These ideas originate in Rubin’s framework for inference with missing data. With several incomplete variables, the assumptions become more subtle because different missingness patterns can coexist.

What the observed data cannot prove

A relationship between observed predictors and missingness is evidence against a simple MCAR explanation, but it does not prove MAR. MAR and MNAR generally cannot be distinguished from the observed data alone. Subject-matter knowledge, collection-process evidence, external data, and sensitivity analysis are needed.

Pythagoras therefore uses terms such as Random, Patterned, Uncertain, and Insufficient data as operational interpretations of its predictive diagnostics. They should not be relabeled automatically as MCAR, MAR, or MNAR.

5. Ask whether missingness is informative

Missingness is informative for prediction when an indicator of absence helps predict the target in held-out data. This is a different question from the formal mechanism. An indicator can be useful under several mechanisms, and an apparently useful signal can also reveal leakage, a temporary collection artifact, or a process unlikely to persist in deployment.

If the signal is credible, a Boolean missingness indicator—called a shadow variable in Pythagoras—can preserve it after imputation. Unhelpful indicators add complexity and should remain subject to feature selection and validation.

6. Compare training with future use

Ask why values will be missing when the model is used. A training dataset may contain historical gaps that no longer occur, while a complete training variable may be delayed or unavailable at prediction time. The same preprocessing rules, required inputs, and timing cutoff must be enforceable in deployment.

✅ Resolution strategies

Resolution is usually iterative rather than a rigid one-pass sequence. Start with source correction and explicit domain rules, then evaluate removal, indicators, imputation, or native model support in combinations appropriate to the intended analysis.

Prevent information leakage

Any preprocessing step that estimates quantities from data—including medians, category frequencies, neighbors, regression relationships, selected thresholds, or chosen features—must be fitted using training observations only. Place learned preprocessing inside the resampling pipeline so every validation fold is transformed using parameters learned without that fold.

Strategy Most appropriate when Principal risk Learned inside resampling?
Correct the source Missingness or placeholders reflect a repairable collection or parsing problem. A repair rule may silently encode an incorrect assumption. No, when the rule is external and deterministic.
Manual domain rule The absent value has a documented, reproducible interpretation. Inventing information or confusing “unknown” with “not applicable.” No, if fixed independently of the analytical sample.
Missingness indicator Absence itself has stable predictive value. Leakage, unstable operational signals, and unnecessary features. Selection and validation should occur inside resampling.
Variable removal A field is too incomplete, unavailable in deployment, or provides insufficient value. Loss of useful signal or unfair exclusion of a subgroup. If chosen from outcome performance, tune inside resampling.
Observation removal The final procedure deliberately declines very incomplete cases or complete-case analysis is defensible. Reduced precision, changed population, and selection bias. Evaluate the rule within the validation design.
Learned imputation Missing predictors can be estimated well enough for the intended model. Leakage, distorted distributions, and false certainty. Yes.
Native model handling A particular estimator has documented missing-value support. Assuming support or behavior shared by an entire model family. The estimator is fitted within resampling.

Correct the source and apply domain rules

The best missing value is often one that can be recovered correctly. Fix parsing, joins, extraction failures, and confirmed placeholders before statistical imputation. Deterministic manual filling should be reserved for rules supported by the variable’s definition and applicable at prediction time.

Do not equate empty, zero, normal, unknown, and not applicable without evidence. A convenient constant can change the meaning and distribution of a variable even when it removes every missing marker.

Add missingness indicators

Create indicators before imputation so that the original absence remains observable. Validate their contribution in held-out data and investigate whether the relationship is plausible and stable. If rows with any missingness will always be deleted, their indicators become constant and provide no information.

Remove variables or observations

Variable removal can be reasonable when coverage is poor and the field contributes little, or when it will not exist in deployment. Observation removal can be reasonable under a carefully defined complete-case or abstention policy. Neither strategy has a universal threshold.

When both are considered, examining variable removal first can preserve more observations, which is why the Excessive missingness card applies actions in that order. Nevertheless, compare the retained population before and after either action. A high-performing model for a narrowed population may not meet the original objective.

Apply learned imputation

Simple imputation estimates one value per variable, such as a median or most frequent category. Multivariable methods use relationships among predictors. More complexity is justified only when held-out performance and downstream validation improve sufficiently.

Evaluation by artificially hiding observed values is useful but imperfect: it assumes those values resemble the genuinely missing cases. That assumption is especially doubtful when missingness is patterned or MNAR is plausible. Single imputation also treats estimated values as known; analyses requiring uncertainty estimates may need multiple imputation or a model designed for incomplete-data inference.

Learned imputation can still be necessary when training data happen to be complete but future predictors may be missing. The fitted strategy must define how those future cases are transformed.

Use native model handling carefully

Missing-value support is implementation-specific. Some estimators reject missing values, some route them during tree construction, and some software silently drops incomplete rows before fitting. Check the exact estimator and version rather than assuming that every “tree” or “linear” model behaves alike.

Native support does not remove the need to identify placeholders, consider structural absence, assess informative missingness, or compare the population of usable predictions.

A practical sequence is to confirm placeholders, inspect prevalence and patterns, investigate predictability and informativeness, and only then choose removal, indicators, imputation, or native handling. Revisit earlier decisions when a later result exposes an implausible assumption.

⚠️ Common pitfalls

  • Treating a placeholder as a real value — or a real value as a placeholder.
  • Calling predictive missingness MAR or MNAR without supporting assumptions.
  • Fitting an imputer or choosing features before cross-validation.
  • Measuring missingness only after incomplete observations have been removed.
  • Replacing structural “not applicable” values with an arbitrary statistic.
  • Assuming complete-case analysis preserves the intended population.
  • Assessing imputation only by whether missing counts fall to zero.
  • Assuming training-time and prediction-time availability are identical.
  • Comparing models after each has silently used a different subset of observations.
